Most creative directors learn the craft first and the people part later. I learned them side by side, in rehearsal rooms, on tour, in the studio, and then building a company out of an empty folder. I can find the idea. I can also get fifty people to ship it.

The story so far, in three acts

The stage taught me what makes people lean in.The classroom taught me what makes them stay.The studio taught me how to get it made.

I

The stage

Age five to the international circuit

Trained in ballet to a professional level as a kid. Conservatory piano from age five. LaGuardia High School for acting. Berklee for music. After that I toured internationally as a musician, released records as an artist, and produced them for other people.

It left me with taste, timing, and how to hold a room that doesn't owe me its attention.

II

The classroom

Coinbase

I joined Coinbase as customer support was being built, next to people who had done it for decades. The product changed every week, so I built the education around it: teaching crypto to employees who had never touched it, onboarding, career coaching. That internal teaching helped lead to Learn with Coinbase, the public education platform.

It left me with how to make a hard subject feel like something you want to finish. That is the whole promise of a certificate.

III

The studio

Co-founder, stealth game studio

I co-founded a game studio and grew it from nothing to about 30 people and 25 contractors. I found and closed every one of them myself: art, marketing, growth, community, engineering. Much of the team works for a share of what we build, so nobody follows a creative direction they don't believe in. I also run the money side: P&L, fundraising, and the decks that go with it.

It left me with how to lead people who don't report to me, and how to make the call on 80 percent of the information.

Intermission: the day job

Twelve years in People Operations, at companies from 20 to 400 people. It looks like a detour. It's the reason my creative work actually ships.

I know how teams really move, where projects stall, and how to build the system so the next one moves faster. At a crisis communications firm I automated my own role two years in a row and spent the freed time on bigger problems.

A creative director spends half the week on the work and half on the people and process around it. I've already done the second half for a decade.

Prove it in the room

An unsolicited spec campaign for MasterClass Certificates. Not affiliated, made to show how I think.

A subscription sells access. A certificate sells a change other people can see. Every course ends in a capstone you can show an employer, so the story ends there too: in the room where the learner shows it.

Learn it from the best.
Prove it in the room.The 60-second trailer, start to finish. Then the rest of the system.

15 hours in 15 seconds

The vertical cutdown. One frame for every hour of the course: the learner's notebook filling up page by page, landing on the finished capstone. Built to loop, so the last frame becomes the reason to watch it again.

Lifecycle that follows the capstone

Emails timed to progress, not the calendar. Each one sounds like the instructor and pulls toward the moment the work gets seen. Hour six is where people quit, so hour six gets the best message.

Hour 1. You know something you didn't this morning.Here's the one idea to hold on to from today.
Hour 6. The middle is where most people stop.What your instructor did when they hit this point.
Capstone unlocked.Make something you'd be proud to show a stranger.
Your certificate is ready to be seen.Add it to your profile. Then go walk into the room.
